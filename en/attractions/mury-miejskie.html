Sights and history

Medieval City Walls

The first mention of Poznań's defensive walls comes from a document of Władysław the Elbow-high of 1297, and their construction is attributed to Przemysł II (the year 1275 is assumed). The walls were 2,300 paces, or about 1,725 m, long, up to 11 m high, with about 35 towers and 4 gates. Only fragments survive today.

  • Hours Remains of the walls in the city: open around the clock
  • Tickets Admission: free

History

The defensive system was modernised several times and took its final shape at the beginning of the 16th century. The walls enclosed about 21 hectares of the town.

The gates were the Wrocław Gate (to the south), the Water and Great Gates (to the east) and the Wronki Gate (to the north). Several pedestrian posterns were also cut into the walls, mainly in the 15th and 16th centuries. The towers were named after the guilds to which they were assigned, e.g. the Tailors' and the Butchers' Tower.

In the 17th and 18th centuries the walls lost their military significance, were partly damaged in wars and repaired ad hoc. They hindered the growth of the town, so in 1797 the Prussians began to demolish them, and this continued until the middle of the 19th century.

The walls are best seen in the view of the town by Braun and Hogenberg of 1618. On its basis a model at a scale of 1:150 was made, which can be seen in the basement of the Franciscan monastery.

What to see

The corner tower by ul. Masztalarska and ul. 23 Lutego, the best-known surviving fragment. The wall here is about 1.5 m thick, and the tower has an outer diameter of 8.5 m, a height of 6 m and eight loopholes.

The fire brigade courtyard on ul. Wolnica: an uncovered, partly reconstructed fragment of the inner wall and Catherine's Tower (Baszta Katarzynek). The tower survived because it belonged to the Church. It stands in the former courtyard of the Catherine nuns' convent, today Salesian. After conservation work you can see the tower and the reconstructed walls.

A fragment of the outer wall by ul. Ludgardy.

Between Chopin Park and ul. Wrocławska a fragment of wall with loopholes from the 18th century, built slightly south of the medieval wall pulled down when the Jesuit monastery was built. A gate towards Wrocław stood here.

Good to know

Red paving sets in the roadway of ul. Wrocławska mark the course of the medieval wall. Similar red markings of walls and gates are in the paving of ul. Jaskółcza, Paderewskiego, Szkolna, Wroniecka, Wielka and Wodna.

Ul. Murna was laid out along the line of the inner wall, and Aleje Marcinkowskiego, the first public promenade in the Polish lands, was created west of the demolished part of the walls.
